# Coffee Making Troubleshooting — Self-Assessment

This short assessment checks how well you understand coffee making troubleshooting and can apply it to real decisions. Answer each question in your own words. Aim to explain your reasoning, not just give a one-word answer.

---

## Q1. Core habit (Understand)
In coffee troubleshooting, why is it important to change only one variable at a time when a cup disappoints? Explain in one or two sentences.

## Q2. Vocabulary and control (Understand)
List the main practical levers a home coffee maker can actually control when troubleshooting a bad cup, and briefly state why keeping this short list in mind is useful.

## Q3. Grind effects (Understand/Apply)
Describe what tends to happen to the taste of coffee when the grind is (a) too coarse and (b) too fine, and explain the reason behind each result.

## Q4. The chain idea (Understand)
The course describes coffee preparation as a connected chain of steps. Explain what this means and how it guides the order in which you troubleshoot a bad cup.

## Q5. Diagnose a scenario (Analyze)
You brew a pot of drip coffee that tastes thin and slightly sour. The beans are one week old and stored in a sealed container. Working backward through the troubleshooting steps, identify the most likely cause and state the single change you would make before the next pot. Justify your answer.

## Q6. Match method to problem (Analyze)
You make a French press using a fine grind that you normally use for espresso, and the result is harsh and bitter. Diagnose why this happens and recommend one adjustment. Explain why your adjustment fits this brewing method.

## Q7. Cleaning as a suspect (Analyze/Apply)
A cup tastes stale and bitter in a way that fresh beans and a correct grind cannot explain. What is the next logical suspect, and why can this problem occur no matter how fresh your beans are?

## Solution

# Teacher Answer Key — Coffee Making Troubleshooting

Use these model answers to score learner responses. Award credit for reasoning that matches the ideas below, even if wording differs.

## Q1 (Understand)
Model answer: Coffee preparation is a chain of connected steps, so a single problem can have several causes. If you change the grind, the coffee amount, and the water all at once, you will not know which change actually fixed or worsened the cup. Changing one variable at a time isolates the cause and makes the process repeatable and controllable. (Grounds: cl1.)

## Q2 (Understand)
Model answer: The main controllable levers are (1) the beans and how they are stored, (2) the grind, (3) the brewing method, and (4) cleaning and maintaining equipment. Reliable consumer guidance groups these as brewing techniques and tips covering everything from grinding beans to cleaning equipment. Keeping this short list in mind gives a ready checklist to run through whenever a cup disappoints. (Grounds: cl2.)

## Q3 (Understand/Apply)
Model answer: (a) Too coarse → weak, watery, or sour coffee, because water rushes through the grounds too quickly and under-extracts. (b) Too fine → bitter, harsh coffee, because water lingers and over-extracts. Adjusting the grind is often the single most effective troubleshooting move. (Grounds: cl3.)

## Q4 (Understand)
Model answer: The chain idea means each stage sets up the next — harvesting and processing affect how coffee should be roasted, and roasting affects how it should be ground and brewed. When troubleshooting, you work backward through that chain to find the link that needs adjusting, which is why the ordered path is beans/storage → grind → method → cleaning. (Grounds: cl4.)

## Q5 (Analyze)
Model answer: Because the beans are fresh (one week old) and well stored in a sealed container, freshness is fine, so that is not the cause. Thin and sour points to the grind: a grind that is too coarse lets water pass too quickly and under-extracts, producing weak, watery, sour coffee. The single change is to grind a little finer for the next pot and keep everything else the same, then taste again. (Grounds: cl3, cl5.)

## Q6 (Analyze)
Model answer: A French press relies on extended contact between water and grounds (a long steep). A fine espresso grind over-extracts during that long steep, producing harsh, bitter coffee. The correct single adjustment is to grind coarser for the French press. This fits the method because you are matching grind size to the way the method extracts, rather than blaming the beans. (Grounds: cl3, cl6.)

## Q7 (Analyze/Apply)
Model answer: The next logical suspect is equipment cleaning. Old coffee oils and residue build up on equipment and add stale, bitter off-flavors to every cup, so even perfectly fresh beans and a correct grind cannot prevent the off taste. Cleaning the equipment is the sensible next change. (Grounds: cl7.)

**Scoring guidance:** Q1–Q4 assess recall and explanation; award partial credit for correct core idea with incomplete reasoning. Q5–Q7 assess analysis and transfer; full credit requires both a correct diagnosis and a justified single next change consistent with the troubleshooting order.
