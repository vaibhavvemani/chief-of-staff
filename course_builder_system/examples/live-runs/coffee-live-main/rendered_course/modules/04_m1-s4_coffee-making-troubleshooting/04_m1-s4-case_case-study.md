# Case Study: Diagnosing the Café's Complaint Board

## Slide 1 — Title
**The Monday Morning Mystery: Troubleshooting a Batch of Bad Cups**

A hands-on case that walks through diagnosing coffee problems one variable at a time.

---

## Slide 2 — The Setting

Imagine a small home-based coffee enthusiast, Dana, who has recently started making coffee for a weekend gathering of friends. Over one weekend, several guests leave comments on a little chalkboard Dana set out: "too sour," "weak and watery," and "bitter, almost burnt."

Dana is new to the craft and wants a reliable way to figure out what went wrong—rather than guessing and hoping the next pot is better. This case follows Dana's reasoning as a model for the troubleshooting mindset.

*(This scenario is illustrative, built to apply the troubleshooting process.)*

---

## Slide 3 — The Framework Dana Uses

Dana remembers a key idea: coffee preparation is a connected chain of steps. How beans are extracted, dried, roasted, and stored all play a role in coffee's taste, texture, and strength. Because harvesting and processing shape how coffee should be roasted, ground, and brewed, each stage sets up the next.

So Dana decides to work backward through the chain and change only one thing at a time.

The checklist:
1. Beans and storage
2. Grind
3. Method
4. Equipment cleaning

---

## Slide 4 — Complaint 1: "Weak and Watery" (Drip)

Dana's first pot was drip coffee, and it tasted thin and slightly sour.

**Working backward:**
- Beans were purchased days earlier and kept sealed—freshness checks out. Since storage and shelf life affect the cup, Dana rules this out.
- That points to grind. A coarse grind lets water rush through too quickly, producing weak, watery, or sour results.

**The single decision:** grind a little finer for the next drip pot, and change nothing else. Grinding is one of the core brewing techniques covered in reliable brewing guidance, and adjusting it is often the most effective move.

---

## Slide 5 — Complaint 2: "Bitter, Almost Burnt" (French Press)

Dana's French press came out harsh and bitter. The beans were the same fresh batch, so freshness is not the culprit.

**The clue:** Dana had ground fine because that setting worked earlier for espresso. But French press relies on extended contact between water and grounds, so a fine grind over-extracts during the long steep and turns bitter.

**The single decision:** grind coarser for the press—matching the adjustment to the method rather than blaming the beans. Popular methods like drip, pour-over, espresso, French press, and cold brew each have their own rhythm.

---

## Slide 6 — Complaint 3: "Off" Flavor Grind Can't Explain

One guest simply wrote that the coffee tasted "stale" in a way that did not match sourness or bitterness. Dana had already confirmed fresh beans and an appropriate grind.

**Next logical suspect:** the equipment. Cleaning equipment is explicitly part of standard brewing guidance, alongside techniques from grinding beans to maintenance. Old oils and residue build up and add stale, bitter off-flavors to every cup regardless of bean freshness.

**The single decision:** clean the brewer thoroughly before the next round.

---

## Slide 7 — Why This Ordered Approach Works

Dana never changed two things at once. Each complaint became a chain to trace: beans → grind → method → cleaning. Because coffee is a chain where each step influences the next, this ordered path reveals the real cause instead of leaving Dana guessing.

Dana also knew where to go deeper: consumer-focused resources like AboutCoffee.org and long-standing education libraries like CoffeeResearch.org—founded in 1999 as the first website dedicated to coffee education—cover brewing methods, grinding, roasting, and storage in more detail.

---

## Slide 8 — Takeaways

- Troubleshooting is a repeatable skill: notice the fault, trace it backward, adjust one variable, taste again.
- Grind is the most common culprit—coarse tends toward weak/sour, fine toward bitter.
- Match the fix to the method; the same grind that suits espresso can ruin a French press.
- When grind and freshness can't explain an "off" taste, suspect dirty equipment.
- The payoff is confidence: a deliberate, informed decision about the one thing to change—and a lesson learned either way.
