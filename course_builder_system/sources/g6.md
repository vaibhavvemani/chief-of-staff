## Curated Source Notes

The article describes the CRO role while it was still spreading beyond its
early financial-services adopters. It attributes the development of the role to
the need for a holistic view of risk exposure and for risk information
aggregated across previously separate business and risk functions.

Key grounding points:

- U.S. financial-services institutions led adoption in the mid-1990s.
- James Lam is credited with defining and developing the CRO role when GE
  Capital hired him in 1993 to establish a new capital-markets business.
- The role integrates credit, market, and operational risk instead of managing
  them as disconnected silos.
- Raising risk management to a C-level position makes it part of executive
  decision-making rather than only a back- or middle-office function.
- The article links adoption to demand from shareholders, regulators, and
  senior managers for risk information aggregated across the business.

Short quoted anchor:

> "Credit risk, market risk, operational risk, are highly related"

## Source Evidence Locators

- Article lines 83-88: mid-1990s adoption, Lam's 1993 appointment, and demand
  for aggregated risk information.
- Lines 89-93: the risk categories integrated by a CRO, elevation to the
  executive level, and the portfolio view of related risks.

## Claims This Source Can Support

- The formal CRO role emerged in the early 1990s and was not established in
  most institutions before then.
- Financial-services firms led broader CRO adoption during the mid-1990s.
- The CRO role responds to the need to manage interacting risk categories at
  institution level.
- Executive risk leadership is intended to aggregate information and connect
  risk management to senior decision-making.
