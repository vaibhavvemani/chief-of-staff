Used for: defining measurable risk vs unmeasurable uncertainty

## Curated Source Notes

Knight's distinction is the conceptual basis for explaining why financial risk
management works only when uncertainty can be measured or reasonably estimated.

Key grounding points:

- "Risk" is uncertainty that can be measured or assigned probabilities.
- "Uncertainty" in the Knightian sense is not measurable in the same way.
- Financial risk management mainly operates in the measurable-risk zone.
- Model-based tools such as VaR, stress testing, scenario analysis, credit
  models, and hedging need some estimate of probability, exposure, or impact.
- If probabilities cannot be estimated, the issue becomes judgment-heavy
  uncertainty rather than ordinary quantifiable risk.

Short quoted anchor:

> "risk means ... a quantity susceptible of measurement"

## Primary-Source Evidence Locators

The online edition reproduces Knight's 1921 public-domain text. In Part I,
Chapter I, paragraphs 223-226, Knight distinguishes measurable risk from
non-quantitative uncertainty and reserves "uncertainty" for the latter. This is
the authoritative passage for the distinction used here; the notes above are a
curated interpretation, not a substitute citation.

## Claims This Source Can Support

- Risk and uncertainty are not the same.
- In financial risk management, risk usually means measurable uncertainty.
- Some future events cannot be reduced to reliable probabilities.
- Quantitative risk tools are useful only inside the measurable-risk zone.
- Human judgment remains important when uncertainty cannot be reliably modeled.
